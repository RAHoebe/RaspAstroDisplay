Make yourself at home

The panel has five main tabs: Tonight, Moon, Weather, Targets and Captures. The top bar holds night mode and Settings. You can use the physical touchscreen or open the same application in a browser on your phone or computer.

In Settings → Location & language, search for your observing location and choose English (US) or Dutch. New installations use Greenwich and English. Times follow the location’s time zone; the interface language controls date and number formatting. Your own notes and telescope names stay as entered.

An attentive, quieter display

Settings has four sections: Location & language, Display, Telescopes, System & backup. Under Display, set separate active and idle brightness from 5–100% in steps of five; idle cannot exceed active. Automatic dimming defaults to 60 seconds of inactivity and 10% idle brightness. An upgrade preserves your existing active brightness; a new installation defaults to 70%.

The Pi backend owns the timer. Only input in its local kiosk counts; viewing from another device, uploads, polling and calculations do not keep the panel awake. The first touch on a dimmed screen only wakes it, including above an open dialog. A second touch operates the interface. It fades up in about 0.2 seconds and down in about one second. Restarting starts at active brightness.

The timeout accepts 10–3600 seconds, and automatic dimming can be disabled. Settings are saved only when changed. The profile is included in ZIP backups. For integrations, GET /api/settings exposes display.profile with active, idle, timeout and auto_dim. Send partial profile updates as {"display":{"idle":10,"timeout":60}} to POST /api/settings with the normal X-Astro-Token. Legacy {"brightness":65} remains supported. Runtime state is available from GET /api/display; activity POSTs additionally require a loopback client address. Devices without a writable backlight keep normal touch behavior and show disabled brightness controls. Red night mode is a separate viewing filter and also covers altitude charts and both comparison images; downloads retain their original colors.

Read the night

Tonight brings together darkness, Moon illumination and a suggested two-hour weather window. Use it as a starting point, then check individual targets. The selected night advances at sunrise, including when you open the dashboard in the morning. Before sunrise, it still shows the remaining current night. Home, targets and altitude tracks share that selection. Recommendations never offer elapsed intervals. Without a sunrise, a labeled noon boundary is used. The status line shows when astronomy, weather and satellite information were updated; cached or stale data are marked.

Moon shows calculated phase, illumination, altitude, direction and upcoming events. Its disk is a schematic phase illustration without surface detail or libration.

Coming night is the default forecast period: sunset to sunrise in at most eight blocks, usually one, two or three hours each. The last block can be shorter. Means are weighted by elapsed time, and block widths match the time axis. Very long polar calendar periods can need a longer block. A separate strip marks astronomical darkness (Sun below −18°). Missing hours are hatched and remain unknown; stale or incomplete data cannot provide reliable weather advice. Next 8 hours starts at the current hour. Both Home and Weather share this choice for the browser session. Weather shows matching interval averages and the cloud map. Expand Weather now to inspect current temperature, humidity, wind and precipitation separately from the selected night. The animated Meteosat layer shows infrared cloud-top temperatures, especially useful around Europe and Africa. It is not precipitation radar and does not measure astronomical seeing. A forecast cannot account for every local cloud.

Choose a promising target

Select your telescope, then choose a sorting method: Recommended, Altitude now, Night altitude, Largest first, Smallest first or Brightest first. Filters offer the coming night, objects currently above the horizon or the next 24 hours; select nebulae, galaxies, star clusters or bright planets, and search by name or catalog identifier.

The 12,160-object OpenNGC selection is a broad catalog, not a promise that every entry is practical for a smart telescope. The visible list changes with location, season, time and the selected observing period. The Moon always has its own shortcut. Venus, Jupiter and Saturn, are included when the altitude and solar-separation thresholds are met.

Recommendations combine altitude and duration (40%), frame coverage (30%) and brightness (30%), with penalties for very low or very small targets. Unknown measurements get a neutral score and an explicit label. The reasons beside each target explain the recommendation; it is not a success percentage. See the calculation notes for limits.

Tap Favorite in a target viewer to save that object for the selected telescope. Favorites only and Not captured with this telescope help build a personal observing list. Capture counters also belong to a specific object and telescope and exclude Trash.

Understand the frame

Open a target to see a survey image with a rectangular field of view for one exposure. Switch between Zoom to target and Zoom to field of view. The last telescope you selected is remembered. In Settings → Telescopes, add up to 12 custom telescopes with a name and explicit horizontal and vertical field in degrees.

Presets are approximately 3.19° × 1.79° for DWARF II and 1.37° × 2.44° for Seestar S50 Pro. They describe nominal, single-exposure telephoto fields. Stacking and cropping can reduce the usable area. Mosaics are outside this view.

The frame is illustrative: the application does not rotate a telescope camera or calculate its current alt-az/EQ orientation. Seestar EQ use does not turn this overlay into a live camera-angle prediction. A solved personal photo, in contrast, has an actual measured orientation.

The percentage compares the object’s longest catalog dimension with the frame’s long edge. It is not an area percentage. Faint extensions may reach farther than the catalog size; unknown sizes remain explicitly unknown.

Choose an imaging window

Inside a target, open Altitude & time. The graph plots the target and Moon through the observing night. Dark shading marks the Sun below −12°, the dashed line marks 30° altitude, and the top bars show forecast cloud cover.

A suggested 30–120 minute window requires the target to stay at least 30° high while the Sun is below −12° at each sampled point. Its score considers altitude, duration, moonlight and available cloud forecasts. Missing weather is marked as uncertain, not interpreted as clear sky.

Positions are sampled at ten-minute intervals. Trees, buildings, local light pollution, seeing and transparency are not modeled. Check the real horizon and sky before committing an exposure.

Build your photo library

Choose Add capture in a target, or Upload on the Captures tab. Assign an object and telescope, select one or more JPG/JPEG or PNG files, and optionally add a capture date and note. The limit is 25 MiB and 32 megapixels per photograph.

Files come from the device running your browser. Open the Pi’s address on your phone or computer to upload photos from there. The panel does not automatically read your telescope app.

Original bytes are preserved. Previews respect EXIF orientation; downloading Original returns the unchanged file. Exact duplicates for the same object and telescope are rejected. Removing a custom telescope preserves its existing photographs and historical name.

Browse Captures by telescope or search term. Open a photo to enlarge it, move through the results, download it, compare it or return to its target. Trash removes a photo from active counts, and the Trash view lets you restore it.

Compare the same sky

With the optional solver installed, open a deep-sky photograph and choose Compare → Align locally. ASTAP matches the stars on the Pi and determines position, pixel scale and orientation. The app fetches a survey reference from CDS and uses Astropy WCS to reproject it into the solved frame, including rotation and reflection.

Use the swipe divider or blink between images. This is a geometric comparison, not a color or exposure match: different filters, resolution, sensitivity and processing produce different-looking images. The website’s interactive example uses Ron’s real solved NGC 7000 capture.

Your photograph stays on the Pi. Only the sky coordinates and requested reference projection are sent to CDS. Comparison works on a separate copy, at most 1600 pixels on the long edge; your original is not overwritten. Successful comparisons are cached.

One full-field DWARF II example took about 41 seconds on the tested Pi 4, including the survey download. This is one measurement, not a speed guarantee. Jobs run one at a time. Heavy crops, sparse stars, blur and star removal can prevent a solution; Moon- or planet-only images are unsupported. An unsolved image remains available in your library.

Download a ZIP backup

Open Settings → System & backup → Create ZIP backup, wait for it to finish, then choose Download ZIP. Save that download on another device. The Pi retains the two most recent generated archives.

The ZIP contains originals, previews and thumbnails including Trash, a consistent SQLite database snapshot, favorites, location, telescope profiles, language and brightness. A manifest includes file sizes and SHA-256 checksums.

Generated comparisons, sky caches, downloaded catalogs, browser-only selections, the OS and desktop/boot configuration are excluded. This is an application backup, not a whole-card image.

Restore to a new directory first

On your Raspberry Pi
.venv/bin/python tools/restore-backup.py /path/to/astro-backup.zip "$HOME/astro-restored"

The restore tool checks paths, checksums and database integrity and refuses to overwrite an existing destination. Stop astro-panel, preserve the current ~/.local/share/astro-panel under a dated name, move the verified restore into its place, then restart the service. Use the desktop user who installed the app. Missing sky caches download again after startup.

Local by design

Installed photographs and settings live in ~/.local/share/astro-panel, outside the code checkout. The browser profile lives in ~/.config/astro-chromium. Astronomy runs locally after the first JPL ephemeris download. Weather, place search, new survey references and new satellite frames require internet access.

The app is intended for a trusted local network and has no user accounts. Anyone who can reach it can view photos and change settings; do not publish port 8080 directly on the internet. Same-origin checks and a per-process token guard cross-site writes.

This documentation site is static. It does not connect to your Pi, access your photographs or run the astronomy backend. It uses no analytics, external fonts or JavaScript CDN. GitHub provides the hosting and may process ordinary request logs under its own privacy policy.
